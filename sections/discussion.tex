\section{Data}
In this project, publicly available person \acs{ReID} datasets were used, due to strict privacy regulations regarding personal data. This highlights a common challenge in the \acs{ReID} field, specifically that these existing datasets often reflect specific cultural, environmental, and geographic contexts. For example the Market-1501, \acs{CUHK03}, and \acs{MSMT17} datasets are all recorded at different universities in different regions of China, while the \acs{DukeMTMC} dataset is recorded at a University in North Carolina, USA. This introduces a potential cultural bias toward the appearance and behavior of university students, as well as a geographical bias, since all of the data is collected from campus environments. Moreover, the majority of the data originates from China, which may limit the models generalizability to populations in other countries. The \acs{iLIDS-VID} dataset is captured at an airport in the United Kingdom, which introduces an entirely different environment. It includes challenges such as occlusions, crowding, carrying luggage, varying clothing styles, and is solely in an indoors environment. This makes it valuable for testing generalization beyond university settings. 
\\\\
The data was split so that the models were trained on the Market-1501, \acs{MSMT17}, and \acs{CUHK03} datasets, and final testing was performed on the \acs{iLIDS-VID} and \acs{DukeMTMC} datasets. This design creates a cross-domain evaluation setup, which is useful for assessing generalization. However, it also introduces a domain mismatch. The model is trained to identify university students on campuses in China, and then tested on different demographic groups in the forms of university students on campus in the USA, and more diverse people inside of an airport. This mismatch likely impacts performance, and raises questions about the fairness and robustness of the system in deployment scenarios. 
\\\\
Furthermore, the datasets are not balanced in size. For example the \acs{MSMT17} dataset contains almost three times more identities and nearly ten times more images than \acs{CUHK03}, which may cause the model to over-represent the dominant dataset and under-represent features from the smaller datasets. In future work, a more structured training strategy could be explored, such as training on each dataset separately, balancing datasets in each batch, or incorporating datasets with diverse cultural, environmental and geographical conditions during training, to improve cross-domain generalization and fairness. 
\\\\
Finally it is important to note that the datasets represent relative controlled environments, with no cases of extreme weather, very low-light conditions, or highly crowded events, which are expected to appear in real-world surveillance footage. 

\subsubsection{Data Augmentations} \label{sec: Dataaugmentation discussion}
Data-augmentation is applied to improve model generalizability, by introducing diversity and simulating real-world circumstances. The augmentation pipeline is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normalization 
    \item JPEG compression
    \item Horizontal flipping
    \item Gaussian blur
    \item Gaussian noise
    \item Brightness change
    \item Resizing
    \item Padding
\end{itemize}

\noindent These augmentations were selected with the purpose of improving model performance. JPEG compression is used to simulate the quality loss often seen in real-world surveillance footage, caused by camera hardware and storage limitations. Standard flipping was used to inflate data. Only horizontal flipping was applied, as vertical flipping would not add meaningful information for the real-word domain of \acs{ReID}. Gaussian blur and noise were applied to simulate visual degradation caused by environmental conditions or surveillance hardware. Brightness change was used to simulate lighting conditions at different times of the day. The images were also resized to different scales, and filled with black padding, to simulate variations in distance and camera quality. Padding is only used to secure that the tensors are the same size for stacking. Overall, the augmentations fulfill their purpose and are appropriate for the domain, but datasets with real degradations and a clean ground truth should be used if available. 
\\\\
Despite this, if all the augmentations happened to be applied heavily to a single image, the quality may degrade to a point where it is too difficult for the model to extract meaningful features. This highlights the importance of balancing data augmentation, so realism is preserved without compromising the visual information. During implementation, the Python \texttt{random} library was used to apply random transformations. However, it would have been more appropriate to use PyTorch's own random functions, as they share the same seeding used throughout the framework, and are compatible with GPU training. This ensures better reproducibility and consistency across experiments, especially when using multiple workers and different hardware setups. 

\subsubsection{OOD Dataset}
The \acs{OOD} dataset consists of different augmented versions of the \acs{DukeMTMC} dataset, and the \acs{iLIDS-VID} dataset. It fulfills the purpose of showing the model's performance on unseen data, outside of its distribution. An argument for using \acs{iLIDS-VID} during training, instead of testing, could be made, as adding a completely different environment could decrease the model's specialization to Chinese campus environments. However, this was not chosen in this project, as the \acs{iLIDS-VID} data is small, and therefore more suitable for evaluation purposes.

\subsection{OOD Test DukeMTMC}

The \acs{OOD} test on \acs{DukeMTMC} revealed that the \hyperlink{model:baseline}{Baseline} model retained the highest performance, despite being trained solely on unmodified images. Interestingly, the \hyperlink{model:endtoend}{End-to-end} model surpassed the \hyperlink{model:augmented}{Augmented} and \hyperlink{model:sequential}{Sequential} models by a considerable margin, suggesting that integrating \acs{SR} in an end-to-end framework can positively contribute under moderate domain-shifts. 
\\\\
The results further suggest that not all strategies for improving robustness are equally effective. Simply training the \acs{ReID} module on heavily augmented data or feeding up-sampled images from \acs{SR} without adaption results in performance degradation, as can be seen with the \hyperlink{model:sequential}{Sequential} model. This underlines the importance of joint optimization when integrating additional modules like \acs{SR}. 

\subsection{OOD Scale Test}

One of the most surprising findings came from the scale test. The results showed an increase in performance on scale 0.75 which was unexpected. However, this is assumed to be caused by the presence of differently sized images in the new dataset, compared to those used during training. Since the model is trained with a maximum image size of \textit{128×64}, it is not fully equipped to handle the larger or differently scaled images. Performance drop again across all metrics when testing on scale 0.5, however the results are still higher than those from the full-sized images. This indicates that matching image scale to the training distribution may be more important than preserving high resolution.

\subsection{OOD Compression Test}

The compression test demonstrated a predictable trend, which is all models performance should decrease as the image quality worsened. However, the \hyperlink{model:baseline}{Baseline} model remained the best-performing across all compression levels, followed closely by the \hyperlink{model:endtoend}{End-to-end} model. This suggests that even when compression artifacts are introduced, the model can still extract features for person \acs{ReID}. 

\subsection{OOD Test iLIDS-VID}

The \acs{iLIDS-VID} results represent the most pronounced support for \hyperlink{hypothesis:1}{Hypothesis 1}, as the \hyperlink{model:endtoend}{End-to-end} model achieved the highest Rank-1 accuracy, outperforming all three models including the \hyperlink{model:baseline}{Baseline} that otherwise dominated the \acs{InD} and \acs{OOD} tests. 
\\\\
This result highlight the benefits of jointly learning to enhance and identify. The \hyperlink{model:sequential}{Sequential} model again achieved the lowest results, reinforcing the idea that simply adding enhancements without retraining or fine-tuning are insufficient for improving generalization.

\subsection{EDSR}
\acs{EDSR} was chosen as the \acs{SR} architecture because the model is known for operating in the low-distortion region of the perception-distortion trade-off, as described in Section \ref{subsec:perception-distortion-tradeoff}. This choice aligned well with the project's goal of preserving the details that the \acs{ReID} module relies on, rather than creating visually appealing images that risk introducing artificial details.
\\\\
The independent evaluation of \acs{EDSR} showed that the model learned to upscale images better than simple interpolation. The scratch model achieved a \acs{PSNR} of 28.25 dB compared to 27.15 dB for bicubic interpolation, an improvement of 1.10 dB (Table \ref{tab:edsr_vs_bicubic}). This improvement confirms that \acs{EDSR} provides better reconstruction quality than traditional methods. However, the improvement is relatively modest compared to what larger \acs{EDSR} configurations or more recent \acs{SR} architectures might achieve. The baseline configuration with 64 features and 16 residual blocks was chosen to balance computation time with quality, but this conservative choice may have limited the model's ability to handle more complex degradation patterns.
\\\\
The training strategy comparison yielded an important finding: domain-specific training outperformed transfer learning for this application. The scratch model, trained only on \acs{RELLISUR}, performed 0.16 dB better than the finetuned model on the \acs{RELLISUR} test set. The initial hypothesis was that finetuning on \acs{DIV2K} would improve generalization by exposing the model to diverse, high-quality training data. However, the results showed the opposite effect. The difference between realistic degradations in \acs{RELLISUR} and synthetic bicubic degradations in \acs{DIV2K} was too large, causing the model to adapt to synthetic patterns at the cost of its ability to handle real low-resolution images. The drop in validation \acs{PSNR} from 24.72 dB after scratch training (Figure \ref{fig:rellisur_psnr_curves}) to 24.28 dB after finetuning (Figure \ref{fig:div2k_psnr_curves}) supports this interpretation. This finding suggests that when training \acs{SR} models for specific deployment scenarios, matching the training data characteristics to the target domain is more important than increasing data diversity through synthetic sources.
\\\\
A fundamental limitation of the \acs{EDSR} approach in this project was that the \acs{SR} training was not connected to the \acs{ReID} task. \acs{EDSR} was trained to minimize pixel-wise L1 loss against \acs{HR} ground-truth images, but this objective does not directly optimize for preserving features that are important for person re-identification. The \acs{SR} module had no way of learning which image details are most relevant for \acs{ReID} embeddings. This may explain why the Sequential configuration showed significantly worse \acs{ReID} performance, even though the \acs{SR} module provided measurable \acs{PSNR} improvements. This observation aligns with the point made in Section \ref{subsec:srandreidsota} about the risk of applying off-the-shelf \acs{SR} models directly to person \acs{ReID} problems.
\\\\
The perception-distortion trade-off also sets natural limits on what \acs{EDSR} can achieve. By prioritizing low distortion, the model produces smoother outputs that minimize pixel error, but may lack the sharp details that perceptually focused models create. This is intentional to avoid artificial artifacts, but it also means that the \acs{SR} images do not necessarily look significantly sharper upon visual inspection, even though the \acs{PSNR} values are higher. For the \acs{ReID} application, this conservative approach was appropriate, as false details could mislead the \acs{ReID} module. However, it also means that the \acs{SR} module's contribution to image quality is fundamentally limited by this trade-off.


\subsection{Sequential EDSR and Baseline}
Implementing both models sequentially effectively meant that only \acs{OSNet} had the chance to improve. By only inputting the \acs{HR} \acs{ReID} image, \acs{EDSR} could not learn more than it already knew about general up-scaling. \acs{OSNet} learned how to extract useful embeddings from \acs{SR} images. 

\subsection{End-to-end EDSR and Baseline}

The End-to-end implementation of the \acs{EDSR} and \acs{OSNet} models served it's purpose as intended. The combined forward pass allowed \acs{EDSR} to learn from \acs{OSNet}'s loss, and thereby specialize the up-scaling to assists in better embeddings. However, having no \acs{SR} specific loss, limited this ability. Implementing an auxillary loss, by inputting down-scaled \acs{ReID} data, and comparing it to the original \acs{HR} data, could have improved performance. Therefore this addition should be considered for future work. 

\section{Deployment}

The choice to cloud-deploy the system, is based on ethical considerations. If the system is incorporated directly into local hardware, it would be implied that the captured footage is constantly being analyzed in real time. Such a design effectively normalizes monitoring the citizens at all times, in line with the definition of a surveillance state. Furthermore, when the system is embedded in local hardware, it becomes more difficult to regulate and monitor how and by whom it is used, increasing the risk of misuse or unauthorized access.
\\\\
On the contrary, if the system is deployed in the cloud, it remains inactive until invoked for a specific purpose. This ensures that footage is not automatically processed unless it is necessary and that access can be controlled through authentication and logs.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

The development and deployment of person \acs{ReID} systems raises several ethical concerns. These systems handle sensitive visual data of individuals, often without their explicit consent, which can impact the right to privacy, and freedom of movement. Therefore, ethical constrains were considered in the design and deployment phase. These concerns are defined and discussed as follows:

\subsubsection{Privacy and Consent}
Surveillance of public areas challenges the concept of informed consent, as individuals are often unaware of the monitoring, and do not have the choice of not being recorded. Therefore, such footage must be handled carefully, and person \acs{ReID} systems should be designed to only be activated when specific requirements are met, such as for the purpose of investigating crime. 

\subsubsection{Transparency}
The public should be informed about where, how, and why person \acs{ReID} systems are used. The cloud deployment strategy supports transparency as it allows for monitoring the usage of the systems. 

\subsubsection{Misuse}
One of the more serious issues regarding person \acs{ReID} systems is the potential for misuse. In the wrong hands, such a system can be used to target innocent people based on their race, religion, or political opinions. It can also be used in a surveillance state, which negatively impacts the citizens freedom of speech and freedom of movement. This risk is reduced through the use of authentication and logs.

\subsubsection{Data Security}
As the system handles potentially sensitive data, it is important to ensure data security. This can be done through encryption and should be applied when storing the data as well as for transmitting the data. Furthermore, data should only be kept for as long as necessary, with access limited to authorized and relevant personnel.

\subsubsection{Dataset improvements}
The training datasets used in this project primarily consists of Chinese and American university campus environments, which introduces cultural, geographical, and environmental bias. Future work should incorporate datasets with diverse populations, clothing styles, and demographics representations to ensure fair and robust performance across different deployment contexts. Additionally, datasets capturing challenging real-world scenarios such as extreme weather, heavy rain, fog, and very low-light scenarios should be included to better prepare the model for actual surveillance conditions. Finally, creating a custom dataset specifically designed to match the target deployment environment would allow for more accurate evaluation of system performance under realistic conditions.


\subsubsection{Super-Resolution Enhancements}
The \acs{SR} module could be improved through several approaches. Implementing an auxiliary loss during end-to-end training would allow the \acs{SR} module to optimize for both reconstruction quality and \acs{ReID} performance at the same time, rather then only learning from only learning from the \acs{ReID} loss. The \acs{SR} network could be designed to specifically  enhance discriminative features such as clothing patterns, logos and textures that are particularly relevant for person identification, rather than generic image enhancement. Furthermore, developing a multi-scale \acs{SR} approach that can handle varying input resolutions would improve robustness across different camera qualities and different distances, which are essential for real-world surveillance scenarios. 

\subsubsection{Re-Identification Enhancements}
The \acs{ReID} module training could be improved through systematic experimentation. Comparing different loss functions such as triplet loss versus cross-entropy loss would provide insights into which approach better handles the one-shot and open-set nature of person \acs{ReID}. Investigating different freezing strategies during End-to-end training, such as trying unfreezing layers or trying different learning rates for \acs{SR} or \acs{ReID} modules, could lead to better feature learning. Finally, conducting systematic hyperparameter tuning including batch size, learning rate schedules, and augmentation strengths would help optimize models   performance.
